## Vector Space

*Definition: Vector space is a special type of module where the scalars come from a field. In other words, a vector space is a module over a field.*
```lean
variable {K : Type*} [Field K] {V : Type*} [AddCommGroup V] [Module K V]
```
We see that :
- `AddCommMonoid` in `Module` is replaced by `AddCommGroup` in `VectorSpace`.
- `Semiring` in `Module` is replaced by `Field` in `VectorSpace`.

<br />

Note that `Field` is commutative ring with multiplicative inverses for all non-zero elements. In other words, a field is a commutative ring in which every non-zero element has a multiplicative inverse.

<br />

*Definition: A linear map between two vector spaces is a function that preserves vector addition and scalar multiplication.*
```lean
variable {W : Type*} [AddCommGroup W] [Module K W]
variable (φ : V →ₗ[K] W)
```
We see that :
- both `V` and `W` are vector spaces over the same field `K`.
- $\varphi$ is a linear map from `V` to `W`.
- `→ₗ[K]` means a linear map over the field `K`.

<br />

*Properties of linear map*

```lean
example (a : K) (v : V) : φ (a • v) = a • φ v :=
  map_smul φ a v

example (v w : V) : φ (v + w) = φ v + φ w :=
  map_add φ v w
```

<br />

*Definition: A linear equivalence between two vector spaces is a bijective linear map $f$ that has an inverse $f^{-1}$ which is also a linear map.*

```lean
noncomputable example (f : V →ₗ[K] W) (h : Function.Bijective f) : V ≃ₗ[K] W :=
  .ofBijective f h
```

<br />

*Definition of bi-projection map: A projection map is a linear map that takes a vector in a product space and returns one of its components.*

```lean
-- First projection map
example : V × W →ₗ[K] V := LinearMap.fst K V W

-- Second projection map
example : V × W →ₗ[K] W := LinearMap.snd K V W
```

<br />

*Definition of bi-product map: A product map is a linear map that takes two linear maps and returns a new linear map that maps a vector to the pair of its images under the two linear maps.*

```lean
-- Universal property of the product
example (φ : U →ₗ[K] V) (ψ : U →ₗ[K] W) : U →ₗ[K]  V × W := LinearMap.prod φ 

-- The product map does the expected thing, first component
example (φ : U →ₗ[K] V) (ψ : U →ₗ[K] W) : LinearMap.fst K V W ∘ₗ LinearMap.prod φ ψ = φ := rfl

-- The product map does the expected thing, second component
example (φ : U →ₗ[K] V) (ψ : U →ₗ[K] W) : LinearMap.snd K V W ∘ₗ LinearMap.prod φ ψ = ψ := rfl
```

That is , the product map takes two linear maps `φ` and `ψ` and returns a new linear map that maps a vector `u` to the pair of its images under the two linear maps:
$$
\varphi \bigotimes \psi : U \to V \bigotimes W, \quad u \mapsto (\varphi(u), \psi(u))
$$

Note that `→ₗ` is the composition operator of linear maps.

<br />

*Definition of bi-inclusion map: An inclusion map is a linear map that takes a vector from one vector space and returns it as a vector in a larger vector space that contains the original vector space as a subspace.*
```lean
``-- First inclusion map
example : V →ₗ[K] V × W := LinearMap.inl K V W

-- Second inclusion map
example : W →ₗ[K] V × W := LinearMap.inr K V W
```

<br />

*Defintion of bi-coproduct map: A coproduct map is a linear map that takes two linear maps and returns a new linear map that maps a pair of vectors to the sum of their images under the two linear maps.*

```lean
-- Universal property of the sum (aka coproduct)
example (φ : V →ₗ[K] U) (ψ : W →ₗ[K] U) : V × W →ₗ[K] U := φ.coprod ψ

-- The coproduct map does the expected thing, first component
example (φ : V →ₗ[K] U) (ψ : W →ₗ[K] U) : φ.coprod ψ ∘ₗ LinearMap.inl K V W = φ :=
  LinearMap.coprod_inl φ ψ

-- The coproduct map does the expected thing, second component
example (φ : V →ₗ[K] U) (ψ : W →ₗ[K] U) : φ.coprod ψ ∘ₗ LinearMap.inr K V W = ψ :=
  LinearMap.coprod_inr φ ψ

-- The coproduct map is defined in the expected way
example (φ : V →ₗ[K] U) (ψ : W →ₗ[K] U) (v : V) (w : W) :
    φ.coprod ψ (v, w) = φ v + ψ w :=
  rfl
```

Note that `φ.coprod ψ` maps a tuple `(v, w)` to the sum of `φ v` and `ψ w`:
$$
\varphi \bigoplus \psi : V \bigoplus W \to U, \quad (v, w) \mapsto \varphi(v) + \psi(w)
$$

<br />

*Definition of universal direct sum map: A direct sum map is a linear map that takes a family of linear maps and returns a new linear map that maps a vector in the direct sum of the vector spaces to the sum of its images under the family of linear maps.*

```lean
example (φ : Π i, (V i →ₗ[K] W)) : (⨁ i, V i) →ₗ[K] W :=
  DirectSum.toModule K ι W 
```
That is, the direct sum map takes a family of linear maps `φ` and returns a new linear map that maps a vector in the direct sum of the vector spaces `V i` to the sum of its images under the family of linear maps `φ(i)`:
$$
\bigoplus_{i} \varphi_i : \bigoplus_{i} V_i \to W, \quad \sum_i v_i \mapsto \sum_i \varphi(i)(v_i)
$$

<br />

*Definition of universal direct product map: A product map is a linear map that takes a family of linear maps and returns a new linear map that maps a vector in the vector space to the tuple of its images under the family of linear maps.*

```lean
example (φ : Π i, (W →ₗ[K] V i)) : W →ₗ[K] (Π i, V i) :=
  LinearMap.pi 
```

That is , the product map takes a family of linear maps `φ` and returns a new linear map that maps a vector in the vector space `W` to the tuple of its images under the family of linear maps `φ(i)`:
$$
\bigotimes_i \varphi_i : W \to \prod_{i} V_i, \quad w \mapsto (\varphi(i)(w))_i
$$

<br />

*Definition of universal projection map: A projection map is a linear map that takes a vector in the product space and returns one of its components.*

```lean
-- The projection maps from the product
example (i : ι) : (Π j, V j) →ₗ[K] V i := LinearMap.proj i

-- The inclusion maps into the sum
example (i : ι) : V i →ₗ[K] (⨁ i, V i) := DirectSum.lof K ι V i

-- The inclusion maps into the product
example (i : ι) : V i →ₗ[K] (Π i, V i) := LinearMap.single K V i
```

<br />

*Definition of universal direct sum equivalence: A direct sum equivalence is a linear equivalence that takes a vector in the direct sum of the vector spaces and returns the tuple of its components.*

```lean
example [Fintype ι] : (⨁ i, V i) ≃ₗ[K] (Π i, V i) :=
  linearEquivFunOnFintype K ι V
```

That is, the direct sum equivalence takes a vector in the direct sum of the vector spaces `V i` and returns the tuple of its components:
$$
\varphi : \bigoplus_{i} V_i \to \bigotimes_i V_i, \quad \sum_i v_i \mapsto (v_i)_i     
$$

## Subspace

*Definition: A subspace is a subset of a vector space that is closed under vector addition and scalar multiplication.*

```lean
variable {K : Type*} [Field K] {V : Type*} [AddCommGroup V] [Module K V]

example (U : Submodule K V) {x y : V} (hx : x ∈ U) (hy : y ∈ U) :
    x + y ∈ U :=
  U.add_mem hx hy

example (U : Submodule K V) {x : V} (hx : x ∈ U) (a : K) :
    a • x ∈ U :=
  U.smul_mem a hx
en

noncomputable example : Submodule ℝ ℂ where
  carrier := Set.range ((↑) : ℝ → ℂ)
  add_mem' := by
    rintro _ _ ⟨n, rfl⟩ ⟨m, rfl⟩
    use n + m
    simp
  zero_mem' := by
    use 0
    simp
  smul_mem' := by
    rintro c - ⟨a, rfl⟩
    use c*a
    simp
```

Note that subspace is defined as a `Submodule` in Lean, which is a structure that contains a subset of a vector space and the operations of vector addition and scalar multiplication. The `Submodule` structure has two fields: `carrier`, which is the subset of the vector space, and `add_mem` and `smul_mem`, which are proofs that the subset is closed under vector addition and scalar multiplication, respectively. 

<br />

*Fact1: The preimage of a subspace under a linear map is also a subspace.*

```lean
variable {K : Type*} [Field K] {V : Type*} [AddCommGroup V] [Module K V]

-- ϕ is a linear map from V to W, H is subspace of W, then the preimage of H under ϕ is a subspace of V
def preimage {W : Type*} [AddCommGroup W] [Module K W] (φ : V →ₗ[K] W) (H : Submodule K W) :
    Submodule K V where
  carrier := φ ⁻¹' H
  zero_mem' := by
    -- `map_zero` is a lemma of group homomorphism, maps zero to zero
    rw [Set.mem_preimage, map_zero]
    exact H.zero_mem
  add_mem' := by
    rintro a b ha hb
    simp [Set.mem_preimage] at *
    exact H.add_mem ha hb
  smul_mem' := by
    rintro a x hx
    simp [Set.mem_preimage] at *
    exact H.smul_mem a hx
```

<br />

*Fact2: A subspace of a vector space is itself a vector space.* 

```lean
example (U : Submodule K V) : Module K U := inferInstance
```

<br />

*Fact3: The intersection of two subspaces is also a subspace.*

```lean
example (H H' : Submodule K V) :
    ((H ⊓ H' : Submodule K V) : Set V) = (H : Set V) ∩ (H' : Set V) := rfl
```

<br />

*Fact4: The union of two subspaces is not necessarily a subspace, but the span of the union of two subspaces is a subspace.*

```lean
example (H H' : Submodule K V) :
    ((H ⊔ H' : Submodule K V) : Set V) = Submodule.span K ((H : Set V) ∪ (H' : Set V)) := by
  simp [Submodule.span_union]
```

Proof : The union of two subspaces is not necessarily a subspace because it may not be closed under vector addition or scalar multiplication. However, the span of the union of two subspaces is a subspace because it is defined as the smallest subspace that contains both subspaces, and it is closed under vector addition and scalar multiplication by definition.

<br />

*Fact5: The top subspace of a vector space is the vector space itself, and the bottom subspace of a vector space is the zero vector space.*

```lean
example (x : V) : x ∈ (⊤ : Submodule K V) := trivial

example (x : V) : x ∈ (⊥ : Submodule K V) ↔ x = 0 := Submodule.mem_bot K
```

<br />

*Fact6: If two subspaces are in direct sum then they span the whole space.*

```lean
-- If two subspaces are in direct sum then they span the whole space.
example (U W : Submodule K V) (h : IsCompl U W) :
  U ⊔ W = ⊤ := h.sup_eq_to
```

<br />

*Fact7: If two subspaces are in direct sum then they intersect only at zero.*

```lean
-- If two subspaces are in direct sum then they intersect only at zero.
example (U W : Submodule K V) (h : IsCompl U W) :
  U ⊓ W = ⊥ := h.inf_eq_bot
```

That is, if two subspaces `U` and `W` are in direct sum or complementary, then their sum spans the whole vector space `V`, and their intersection is the zero vector space. This means that every vector in `V` can be uniquely expressed as a sum of a vector from `U` and a vector from `W`, and the only vector that is in both `U` and `W` is the zero vector.

<br />

*Fact8: If subspaces are in direct sum then they span the whole space and pairwise intersect only at zero.*

```lean
-- If subspaces are in direct sum then they span the whole space.
example (U : ι → Submodule K V) (h : DirectSum.IsInternal U) :
  ⨆ i, U i = ⊤ := h.submodule_iSup_eq_top

-- If subspaces are in direct sum then they pairwise intersect only at zero.
example {ι : Type*} [DecidableEq ι] (U : ι → Submodule K V) (h : DirectSum.IsInternal U)
    {i j : ι} (hij : i ≠ j) : U i ⊓ U j = ⊥ :=
  (h.submodule_iSupIndep.pairwiseDisjoint hij).eq_bot

-- The relation with external direct sums: if a family of subspaces is
-- in internal direct sum then the map from their external direct sum into `V`
-- is a linear isomorphism.
noncomputable example {ι : Type*} [DecidableEq ι] (U : ι → Submodule K V)
    (h : DirectSum.IsInternal U) : (⨁ i, U i) ≃ₗ[K] V :=
  LinearEquiv.ofBijective (coeLinearMap U) h
```

Note that internal direct sum means homomorphism $\bigoplus_{i} U_i \rightarrow V$ is bijective.

<br />

*Fact9: If a vector is in the span of the union of two subspaces then it can be expressed as a sum of a vector from each subspace.*

```lean
example {S T : Submodule K V} {x : V} (h : x ∈ S ⊔ T) :
    ∃ s ∈ S, ∃ t ∈ T, x = s + t  := by
  rw [← S.span_eq, ← T.span_eq, ← Submodule.span_union] at h
  induction h using Submodule.span_induction with
  -- x ∈ S ∪ T → ∃ s ∈ S, ∃ t ∈ T, x = s + t
  | mem y h =>
    rcases h with (hy|hy)
    · use y, hy, 0, T.zero_mem; module
    · use 0, S.zero_mem, y, hy; module
  -- 0 ∈ span(S ∪ T) → ∃ s ∈ S, ∃ t ∈ T, 0 = s + t
  | zero =>
    use 0, S.zero_mem, 0, T.zero_mem; module
  -- x ∈ span(S ∪ T), y ∈ span(S ∪ T) → x + y ∈ span(S ∪ T)
  | add x y hx hy hx' hy' =>
    rcases hx' with ⟨s, hs, t, ht, rfl⟩
    rcases hy' with ⟨s', hs', t', ht', rfl⟩
    use s + s', S.add_mem hs hs', t + t', T.add_mem ht ht'; module
  -- x ∈ span(S ∪ T), a : K → a • x ∈ span(S ∪ T)
  | smul a x hx hx' =>
    rcases hx' with ⟨s, hs, t, ht, rfl⟩
    use a • s, S.smul_mem a hs, a • t, T.smul_mem a ht; module
```

*Fact10: A linear map is injective if and only if its kernel is the zero subspace, and a linear map is surjective if and only if its range is the whole space.*

```lean

example : Surjective φ ↔ range φ = ⊤ := range_eq_top.symm
```

*Fact11: The image of a subspace under a linear map is also a subspace, and the preimage of a subspace under a linear map is also a subspace.*

```lean
example (E : Submodule K V) (F : Submodule K W) :
    Submodule.map φ E ≤ F ↔ E ≤ Submodule.comap φ F := by
  constructor
  · intro h x hx
    exact h ⟨x, hx, rfl⟩
  · rintro h - ⟨x, hx, rfl⟩
    exact h hx
```

*Fact12: The quotient of a vector space by a subspace is also a vector space, and the canonical projection from the vector space to the quotient space is a linear map whose kernel is the subspace and whose range is the whole quotient space.*

```lean
-- quotient space V/E
example : Module K (V ⧸ E) := inferInstance

-- the canonical projection from V to V/E
-- `E.mkQ` is the quotient map, which sends each element of V to its equivalence class in V/E
example : V →ₗ[K] V ⧸ E := E.mkQ

-- the kernel of the quotient map is E
example : ker E.mkQ = E := E.ker_mkQ

-- the image of the quotient map is the whole space V/E
example : range E.mkQ = ⊤ := E.range_mkQ
```

Note that the quotient space `V ⧸ E` is defined as the set of equivalence classes of vectors in `V` under the equivalence relation defined by the subspace `E`. The canonical projection `E.mkQ` maps each vector in `V` to its equivalence class in `V ⧸ E`, and its kernel is exactly the subspace `E`, while its range is the whole quotient space.

*Fact13: If a linear map maps a subspace into another subspace, then it induces a linear map between the corresponding quotient spaces.*

```lean
example (hφ : E ≤ ker φ) : V ⧸ E →ₗ[K] W := E.liftQ φ hφ

example (F : Submodule K W) (hφ : E ≤ .comap φ F) : V ⧸ E →ₗ[K] W ⧸ F := E.mapQ F φ hφ
```

## Endomorphism

*Fact1: Endomorphisms of `V` over `K`, multiplication of endomorphisms is composition of linear maps.*

```lean
example (φ ψ : End K V) : φ * ψ = φ ∘ₗ ψ :=
  End.mul_eq_comp φ ψ -- `rfl` would also work
```

<br />

*Fact2: Evaluating a polynomial at an endomorphism gives a new endomorphism.*

```lean
example (P : K[X]) (φ : End K V) : V →ₗ[K] V :=
  aeval φ P 
```

Notice that `aeval φ P` evaluates the polynomial `P` at the endomorphism `φ`, resulting in a new endomorphism of `V`. Where `aeval` stands for "algebraic evaluation", which means plugging a $K$-Algebra into the polynomial. And endomorphism $\varphi$ is treated as an element of the $K$-Algebra of endomorphisms of `V`.

<br />

*Fact3: Criterion for a submodule to be trivial.*

```lean
#check Submodule.eq_bot_iff
```
Note that `\bot` represents the trivial submodule (equal to ⊥). It is the intersection of all submodules of `V` and contains only the zero vector.


<br />

*Fact4: If two polynomials are coprime, then the intersection of the kernels of their evaluations at an endomorphism is trivial.*

```lean

#check Submodule.mem_inf
#check LinearMap.mem_ker

example (P Q : K[X]) (h : IsCoprime P Q) (φ : End K V) : ker (aeval φ P) ⊓ ker (aeval φ Q) = ⊥ := by
  rw [Submodule.eq_bot_iff]
  rintro x hx
  rw [Submodule.mem_inf, mem_ker, mem_ker] at hx
  rcases h with ⟨U, V, hUV⟩
  have := congr((aeval φ) $hUV.symm x)
  simpa [hx]
```
Note that :
- `Submodule.eq_bot_iff` gives a criterion for when a submodule is trivial (equal to ⊥). That is, a submodule `M` is equal to ⊥ if and only if every element `x` in `M` is equal to `0`.
- `Submodule.mem_inf` gives a criterion for when an element belongs to the intersection of two submodules. That is, an element `x` belongs to `M ⊓ N` if and only if it belongs to both `M` and `N`.
- `LinearMap.mem_ker` gives a criterion for when an element belongs to the kernel of a linear map. That is, an element `x` belongs to `ker(f)` if and only if `f(x) = 0`.
- `IsCoprime` gives a criterion for when two polynomials are coprime, meaning there exist polynomials `U` and `V` such that `U * P + V * Q = 1`. It is known as Bézout's identity.
- `aeval φ` is a ring homomorphism from `K[X]` to the endomorphism ring of `V`, 
$
  \text{aeval }\varphi : K[X] \to \text{End}_K(V)
$
That is, it sends a polynomial `P` to the endomorphism `aeval φ P`, and it preserves addition and multiplication of polynomials when evaluated at the endomorphism `φ`.
- `congr (aeval φ) $...` is a tactic used to apply congruence to expressions involving `aeval φ`. It helps in simplifying goals where `aeval φ` is applied to both sides of an equation.

*Fact6: The kernel of a linear map is contained in the kernel of its composition with another linear map.*

```lean
#check LinearMap.ker_le_ker_comp
```

Proof : 
If `x` belongs to `ker(f)`, then `f(x) = 0`. For any linear map `g`, we have `(g ∘ f)(x) = g(f(x)) = g(0) = 0`. Therefore, `x` also belongs to `ker(g ∘ f)`, which shows that `ker(f) ⊆ ker(g ∘ f)`.
